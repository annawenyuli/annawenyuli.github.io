Who Owns the Agent Economy? From Harvey and Sierra to Fireworks, Modal, and Browserbase

A technical and commercial map of enterprise agents, vertical AI, voice, browser runtimes, inference infrastructure, compute, and the expert-data supply chain beneath them.

The agent economy starts when AI can act

The first wave of generative AI products followed a simple loop: question in, answer out. Agents add planning, tools, state, execution and verification. That seemingly small change creates multiple new markets because intelligence now has to touch real systems.

From chatbot to agent

→

→

→
VerifyDid the real-world task succeed?

The market therefore fragments into specialized layers: enterprise agents that own a business outcome, vertical AI systems that encode professional workflows, voice and browser runtimes that let agents operate in the world, infrastructure that serves models cheaply, and training-data companies that teach agents what “good work” looks like.

Sierra: own the customer relationship

Sierra’s ambition is not to be a support chatbot. It wants to be the customer-facing digital employee. That means it must combine knowledge, identity, account context and the ability to perform actions in backend systems.

Its strategic idea is especially interesting because a customer relationship is long-lived. The agent may need to follow up days later, remember a promised action, react to a changed account state, or move between chat, email and voice. The unit is not a single chat session; it is an ongoing customer outcome.

Decagon: encode the company’s operating procedure

Decagon’s important product idea is the Agent Operating Procedure: represent support policy in a form that business teams can author, while retaining deterministic controls underneath. Pure LLM behavior is too probabilistic for enterprise policy; pure code is too rigid for conversational work. AOPs try to bridge the two.

Policy: If order_age < 30 days AND item_status = unopened THEN refund is allowed ELSE explain policy and offer escalation Agent: handles natural-language variation but executes within policy boundaries

Salesforce Agentforce: the incumbent advantage is data gravity

Salesforce can start with customer identity, account state, cases, purchase history, sales history and workflow context already inside the platform. Sierra and Decagon often have to integrate into those systems. This is a substantial distribution and context advantage.

But the incumbent also carries a business-model tension: traditional service software monetizes human seats. A successful agent may reduce the number of human seats required. AI-native companies have no reason to protect that legacy revenue model.

Who owns the enterprise agent layer?

The likely outcome is not winner-take-all. Salesforce-heavy companies may prefer Agentforce because context and governance are already native. Heterogeneous enterprises may prefer an independent agent layer such as Sierra or Decagon because it can span multiple systems and remain neutral.

Harvey: why vertical AI is much more than RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation is essential in legal AI because the model must answer from matter-specific documents, firm knowledge, precedent and policy. But RAG only solves one problem: supplying the right knowledge at inference time.

What RAG does—and what it does not
RAGFind relevant documents and put them into model context.
≠
Complete legal workflowPlan, compare, extract, verify, redline, cite, escalate.

A serious legal agent reviewing thousands of contracts must ingest documents, classify them, detect clauses, reason against policy, extract structured fields, handle exceptions, build a diligence table, preserve citations and support human verification. That is an agentic workflow plus domain evaluation—not simply vector search.

Harvey’s moat compounds through workflow data

Every legal workflow produces signals: where lawyers correct the system, which documents matter, what counts as a dangerous clause, what language is acceptable, which answers fail review and how expert users judge quality. Those signals can become domain-specific evaluations and eventually post-training data.

Does Harvey need MCP?

Not in order to exist, but connectivity is increasingly important. RAG is about knowledge retrieval. MCP and APIs are about connecting tools and systems. A legal agent may need to retrieve from a document-management system, access a data room, write to a matter system or use a research database. The richer the action surface, the more useful standardized connectors become.

Zapier: why it should not necessarily become a personal agent

Zapier’s historical advantage is not intelligence. It is the integration graph: thousands of apps, OAuth connections, triggers, actions, retries, filters, branching, audit trails and workflow state.

A consumer personal agent would put Zapier into direct competition with companies whose main advantages are conversational interface, consumer distribution and personal context. That is not where Zapier is strongest.

Why direct MCP does not eliminate middleware

Direct connectivity solves only part of the problem. Production workflows still need credential storage, access control, event triggers, rate-limit handling, retries, deterministic branches and observability. Some workflows should not ask an LLM to re-reason every time. “If contract is signed, create billing account” is better represented as deterministic automation.

That suggests a layered architecture: the agent handles judgment, MCP standardizes tool discovery, and Zapier or another workflow engine handles reliable multi-system execution.

ElevenLabs: selling the ears, mouth and conversation runtime

Siri and Gemini can absolutely compete in voice. The important distinction is the buyer. Siri is an Apple product for Apple users. ElevenLabs can sell voice infrastructure to an airline, insurer, bank, call center, vertical AI company or personal-agent developer.

The hidden technical problem is turn-taking. Real people pause, interrupt, hesitate and talk over each other. A good voice system must determine whether silence means “I am thinking” or “I am done,” and whether an interruption should immediately stop synthesis. That interaction model is a distinct technical layer, not simply a prettier voice.

ElevenLabs can therefore remain model-agnostic: use OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini or a customer-hosted model as the brain while ElevenLabs owns audio quality, speech recognition, turn-taking and telephony integration.

Fireworks: the intelligence-infrastructure layer between models and applications

Fireworks sits between open/foundation models and AI applications. A vertical company may not want to send every request to a generic frontier API forever. It may want a specialized model, lower inference cost, lower latency, more control and a path to post-training on proprietary data.

Fireworks is not a RAG company

RAG changes what context a model sees at inference time. Fireworks works on the model and serving system itself: fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, quantization, batching, GPU kernels, memory management, parallelism and routing.

Continuous batching

A GPU is expensive. If requests arrive at different times and lengths, naive serving leaves compute idle. Continuous batching keeps inserting active sequences into the GPU workload so utilization remains high.

Quantization

Using lower-precision representations such as FP8 can reduce memory requirements and memory bandwidth. That can increase throughput and lower serving cost if model quality remains acceptable.

KV cache

Transformers repeatedly reference prior context. Caching key/value states prevents the system from recomputing the entire prefix for every next token. Cache placement, eviction and sharing become major systems problems at scale.

Sharding and parallelism

Large models may not fit on one accelerator. Serving them across multiple GPUs requires careful partitioning and fast interconnects. At this level, networking and GPU communication are part of the model business.

Speculative decoding

A smaller or faster model can propose several future tokens, which the larger model verifies in a batch. When the guesses are accepted, the expensive model does less sequential work.

These optimizations explain why inference infrastructure can be a large standalone business. The customer is often spending enough on GPUs that a modest efficiency improvement produces substantial economic value.

Baseten: model → production

A research model running in a notebook is not a production service. Baseten packages dependencies, deploys replicas, manages health, routes traffic, scales with demand and exposes an API. Its key abstraction is the model deployment.

Together AI: open-model cloud

Together spans a wider lifecycle: choose an open model, run serverless inference, fine-tune, train, use dedicated GPUs and move from experimentation into production. The closest analogy is a cloud platform designed specifically around open-model AI workloads.

Modal: compute without managing the cluster

Modal is more general. The workload does not have to be a model. It can be an RL rollout, a video pipeline, image generation, scientific compute, code execution or an agent sandbox. The abstraction is the elastic function/container rather than the model endpoint.

@modal.function(gpu="H100") def run_job(): ... # Conceptually: # request compute → run workload → scale down when finished

Browserbase: why a browser becomes infrastructure

Browser automation looks trivial until it must operate at internet scale. A single developer can launch Chrome. An agent company serving millions of users must manage isolated sessions, cookies, authentication state, proxies, crashes, concurrency, screenshots, recording and debugging.

The longer-term opportunity may be agent identity rather than browser hosting alone. A healthy agentic web needs websites to know which software agent is acting, which user it represents and what permissions it has—rather than every bot pretending to be a human browser.

Mercor vs. Snorkel AI vs. Scale AI

Frontier AI data has evolved from annotation labor into an intelligence supply chain. The model no longer needs a worker merely to label whether an image contains a car. It needs lawyers, doctors, bankers, engineers and consultants who can define expert tasks, judge model work, create rubrics and build realistic environments.

Mercor: start with the expert network

Mercor’s original strength was recruiting, evaluating, matching and paying people. That becomes highly valuable when frontier labs need hundreds of specialized professionals. Mercor can assemble the human network, operationalize the work and turn the resulting expertise into datasets, evaluations and environments.

Snorkel: start with data engineering

Snorkel began from a research idea: instead of labeling every example manually, experts can encode labeling functions or heuristics and software can combine noisy signals into probabilistic labels. Its DNA is therefore “how do we engineer training data efficiently?” rather than “how do we recruit a workforce?”

Scale AI: the first major data infrastructure platform

Scale rose through large-scale annotation for autonomous driving and then expanded into RLHF, expert data, red teaming and evaluations. Its history demonstrates a recurring pattern: the data company follows the frontier of what models need.

From SaaS to Work-as-a-Service

The most consequential business-model change may be the unit of sale. Traditional SaaS sells access to software. Copilots sell productivity. Agents can increasingly sell completed work: a resolved ticket, a qualified lead, a legal review, a code change, a customer conversation, a browser task or a production inference workload.

The economic transition
Software seatPay to use the tool
→
AI usagePay per token / call / minute
→
Digital laborPay per completed task
→
OutcomePay for business result

That changes competitive boundaries across the entire software industry. A company that used to sell a tool can become a labor provider. A cloud provider can become an agent runtime. A data vendor can become a training environment company. A vertical application can begin post-training its own models. The stack is becoming more vertically integrated and more modular at the same time.

The strategic question for every AI company is therefore the same: which layer do we need to own, and which layers should remain interchangeable?
